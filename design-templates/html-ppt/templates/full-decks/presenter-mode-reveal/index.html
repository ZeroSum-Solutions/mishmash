// part 02 · presenter view

A presenter view needs four pieces of information

①
A large current-slide view

Should take up over half the view, so you can tell at a glance what the audience sees right now.

②
A next-slide preview

Helps you pre-plan your transition line, so you're not caught thinking "wait, what was I going to say on the next slide?"

③
The speaker-notes area

Large type, high contrast, scrollable — this is the thing a presenter is actually looking at.

④
Timer + slide number

Knowing how long you've talked and how many slides are left — your whole pacing depends on it.

A presenter view should give you four pieces of information. I'll go through them in order of importance.

First, a large current-slide view. This needs to take up over half the space, because it's your "sync anchor" with the audience — whatever they're seeing, that has to be front-of-mind for you too.

Second, a next-slide preview. A lot of people don't get why this matters, so let me explain: the moment a talk stumbles most isn't while you're on a slide — it's the 2 seconds while you're turning to the next one. If you've already seen what the next slide looks like, the transition line comes naturally.

Third, the speaker-notes area — this is today's main focus, and I'll dig into it in the next section. For now, one hard requirement: the type has to be large, the contrast has to be high, and it has to scroll. Because while you're talking, you only get a glance out of the corner of your eye — if the text is small, there's no time to read it.

Fourth, timer and slide number. Knowing how long you've talked and how many slides remain — your whole sense of pacing depends on this. Keynote does this best; reveal.js's default isn't clear enough.

All four pieces matter — none of them is optional. Today's deck has all four built in; press S and try it yourself.

// part 03 · script

The 3 hard rules for speaker notes

01
Not a verbatim script — a "cue signal"

Bold the core point, break transition lines into their own paragraph, list out data and names clearly — so a glance is enough to pick it back up.

02
150–300 words per slide, no more, no less

Under 150 words isn't enough of a cue; over 300 and you won't have time to read it. Pace it for 2–3 minutes per slide.

03
Write it in spoken language, not written language

"Therefore" → "so"; "the aforementioned approach" → "this approach." Read it back after writing it — it should sound like speech.

Now for the most important part — how to actually write speaker notes. I've boiled it down to 3 hard rules.

Rule one: speaker notes are not a script. A lot of people hear "speaker notes" and assume it means writing out every sentence word for word. Wrong. If you read straight off the page, the audience notices instantly, and trust evaporates.

The real job of speaker notes is to be a "cue signal" — bold the core points, break transition lines into their own paragraph, list out data and proper nouns clearly. That way, a glance is enough to pick it back up, but what comes out of your mouth is still your own words.

Rule two: keep each slide to 150-300 words. This is a number I arrived at after a dozen-plus talks. Under 150 words and you don't have enough of a cue — you stall halfway through; over 300 and there's no way you can scan it in time. At roughly 2 to 3 minutes per slide, this word count is just right.

Rule three: write it in spoken language. This is where most people trip up. You write "therefore," but out loud it becomes "so"; you write "the aforementioned approach," but out loud it becomes "this approach." Read it back after you write it — it should sound natural, not stiff.

Put these three rules together, and presenting a deck suddenly becomes a genuinely comfortable thing to do.
